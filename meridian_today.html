In 1941, the chemist Linus Pauling was bedridden with a kidney disorder when he became fixated on a question: why do sickle-shaped red blood cells only appear in deoxygenated venous blood? His answer — that the hemoglobin molecule itself must change shape — became the first molecular disease ever described. Pauling did not have an electron microscope. He had a question that would not leave him alone.

This morning, the landscape across the six areas that anchor the BD Platform is shaped by a similar restlessness: engineers refusing to accept the limitations of what came before. Xencor reports that their engineered TL1A antibody holds a 74-day half-life — long enough for quarterly dosing in IBD. Cabaletta presents data showing CAR-T cells can reset autoimmunity without lymphodepleting preconditioning. And Windward Bio raises $165M to build an anti-TSLP antibody dosed twice a year. Each of these is a refusal to accept the current dosing paradigm as final. The tea is hot. The questions are better.

TL1A: The Half-Life Race Reshapes the Class

FOCUS AREA: TL1A · IBD (UC + CROHN'S)

At Digestive Disease Week in Chicago on May 4, Xencor presented final Phase 1 results for XmAb942, their engineered anti-TL1A antibody built on the Xtend Fc platform. The headline number: a pooled estimated terminal half-life of 74.1 days, supporting a maintenance dosing interval of once every 12 weeks (Q12W) via a single subcutaneous injection. Quantitative pharmacology modeling indicates XmAb942 may achieve greater than 99% TL1A inhibition in 86% of patients during induction and greater than 90% inhibition in 90% of patients on Q12W maintenance. No serious or severe treatment-emergent adverse events were reported across all dose cohorts.

The immunogenicity profile is worth parsing. At the doses being tested in the ongoing XENITH-UC Phase 2b study, 25% of healthy volunteers tested positive for anti-drug antibodies (ADA), but 0% developed neutralizing antibodies. Across all dose levels, ADA incidence was 57%, but the rate declined at higher exposures — suggesting that sustained drug levels may tolerize the immune response to the therapeutic. This is a favorable signal for a chronic-dosing indication like IBD.

Xencor also disclosed preclinical data on XmAb412, a bispecific antibody targeting both TL1A and IL-23 using their XenLock platform. Preclinical models show potent dual suppression with a predicted human half-life of 60–70 days. First-in-human dosing in healthy volunteers is planned for Q3 2026. If the dual-target hypothesis validates, XmAb412 could collapse two mechanisms (anti-TL1A + anti-IL-23) into a single molecule — a direct threat to combination regimens.

The XENITH-UC Phase 2b trial has enrolled approximately 220 patients across three active arms and placebo. A blinded interim analysis is expected around year-end 2026, with primary endpoint results in H2 2027. Merck's tulisokibart (Phase 3 ATLAS-UC and ARES-CD) and Roche's afimkibart (Phase 3 AMETRINE) remain ahead on enrollment timelines, but neither offers Q12W dosing. The TL1A class is validated. The differentiation now is pharmacokinetic convenience — and Xencor's Xtend platform is purpose-built for that fight.

BD signal: Merck's expansion of tulisokibart into RA, HS, and axial spondyloarthritis via Phase 2b trials signals confidence that TL1A blockade extends well beyond IBD. For Ailux, the question is whether epitope-differentiated or anti-fibrotic-biased TL1A assets command distinct value in licensing conversations, especially as the class moves toward extra-intestinal indications where fibrosis biology matters most.

T-Cell Engineering: Preconditioning-Free CAR-T Arrives

FOCUS AREA: T-CELL ENGINEERING (ACE) · IMMUNE RESET (SLE, MYOSITIS)

At ASGCT 2026 in Boston on May 14, Cabaletta Bio presented data that may quietly redefine the trajectory of cell therapy in autoimmune disease. In the RESET-PV trial, four patients with refractory pemphigus vulgaris received a single infusion of rese-cel (resecabtagene autoleucel), a CD19-directed CAR-T therapy — without any lymphodepleting preconditioning. Two of four patients demonstrated compelling drug-free clinical responses sustained through six months of follow-up.

The translational details matter. CAR-T expansion kinetics in the preconditioning-free (PC-free) cohort were similar in magnitude and timing to patients who received standard lymphodepletion. Three of four patients achieved complete peripheral B cell depletion. Safety was favorable: no dose-limiting toxicities, no immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity (ICANS), and only one case of grade 1 cytokine release syndrome (transient fever). The three patients with full B cell depletion showed the most robust clinical improvements, with reductions in anti-desmoglein-3 and anti-desmoglein-1 autoantibodies.

Why this matters structurally: lymphodepleting chemotherapy (typically fludarabine/cyclophosphamide) is the primary barrier to scaling CAR-T beyond academic medical centers. It requires inpatient monitoring, carries infection risk, and limits the patient population to those fit enough for cytotoxic conditioning. If PC-free CAR-T can achieve equivalent immune reset at optimized doses, the therapy moves from hematology infusion suites to community infusion centers. Cabaletta is now enrolling higher-dose PC-free cohorts in PV, with initial PC-free SLE data expected at EULAR 2026 (London, June 3–6).

Separately, Cabaletta presented the first clinical data from patients treated with rese-cel manufactured on the automated Cellares Cell Shuttle platform — the first use of this automated system in any clinical program. Both patients met all release specifications and showed CAR-T expansion and B cell depletion kinetics consistent with the broader RESET dataset. The Cellares partnership, structured as a 10-year commercial supply agreement, is designed to produce thousands of doses annually with minimal capital investment.

BD signal: The preconditioning-free data fundamentally changes the addressable market calculation for autoimmune CAR-T. If outpatient dosing becomes feasible, deal valuations for CD19 CAR-T autoimmune programs shift from rare-disease economics toward chronic-disease scale. Watch the EULAR PC-free SLE data closely — lupus is the indication where payer and regulatory interest is most advanced.

TSLP: Windward Raises $165M for Twice-Yearly Dosing

FOCUS AREA: TSLP · RESPIRATORY (ASTHMA, COPD)

On May 4, Windward Bio announced a $165M crossover financing round led by OrbiMed, with participation from RA Capital, Janus Henderson, Sanofi Ventures, and Harbour BioMed. The capital backs two programs: WIN378, a fully human anti-TSLP monoclonal antibody engineered for ultra-long-acting pharmacokinetics with twice-yearly (Q6M) dosing, and WIN027, a bispecific targeting both TSLP and IL-13.

WIN378 is in the Phase 2/3 POLARIS study in severe asthma, with the Phase 2 dose-ranging component fully recruited and preliminary data expected H2 2026. The Phase 3 portion is planned to begin Q4 2026. A Phase 2 COPD trial (SIRIUS) is anticipated to start in Q2 2026 — effectively now. The competitive benchmark is Amgen/AstraZeneca's tezepelumab (Tezspire), which requires monthly subcutaneous injection. A Q6M dosing interval, if validated, would represent a step-change in patient convenience and adherence — the two variables that most directly determine real-world outcomes in chronic respiratory disease.

The participation of Sanofi Ventures is worth flagging. Sanofi's own respiratory franchise is anchored by dupilumab (Dupixent, co-developed with Regeneron), which targets IL-4R-alpha. TSLP sits upstream of IL-4 signaling in the type 2 inflammatory cascade. A Sanofi Ventures investment in a next-generation TSLP asset could signal interest in lifecycle positioning — hedging Dupixent's eventual biosimilar exposure with an ultra-long-acting upstream blocker.

BD signal: The TSLP space is bifurcating into short-acting (tezepelumab, monthly) and ultra-long-acting (WIN378, Q6M) tiers. For Ailux's TSLP-area monitoring, the key question is whether half-life engineering alone can differentiate, or whether dual-target approaches (WIN027's TSLP+IL-13; Pfizer's tilrekimig IL-4/IL-13/TSLP trispecific) will define the next competitive frontier.

IL-4Rα: Pfizer's Trispecific Advances Toward Phase 3

FOCUS AREA: IL-4Rα · ATOPY (AD, ASTHMA, COPD)

Pfizer announced positive Phase 2 topline results for tilrekimig, a once-monthly trispecific antibody targeting IL-4, IL-13, and TSLP simultaneously, in adults with moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis (AD). The study met its primary endpoint: a statistically significant increase in the percentage of participants achieving EASI-75 at Week 16 across all doses tested versus placebo. Pfizer is advancing tilrekimig into a pivotal Phase 3 AD study in the second half of 2026, with Phase 2 asthma and Phase 2b/3 COPD trials also underway.

Tilrekimig represents a distinct competitive threat to the IL-4R-alpha area because it collapses three therapeutic targets into a single molecule. Current standard of care in moderate-to-severe AD is dupilumab (Sanofi/Regeneron), which blocks IL-4 and IL-13 via IL-4R-alpha. Tezepelumab (Amgen/AstraZeneca) blocks TSLP in asthma but has no AD indication. A trispecific that addresses both pathways simultaneously could claim superiority on depth of type 2 suppression — particularly in patients with incomplete response to dupilumab.

In parallel, Connect Biopharma's rademikibart, a next-generation IL-4R-alpha antibody with 2-fold stronger binding than dupilumab and an optimized epitope, has completed Phase 3 (RADIANT-AD) with 63% EASI-75 at Week 16. The IL-4R-alpha space is evolving from a Dupixent monopoly into a contested landscape where binding affinity, multi-target coverage, and dosing convenience all serve as axes of differentiation.

BD signal: Pfizer's trispecific approach validates the hypothesis that multi-cytokine blockade can outperform single-pathway inhibition in type 2 inflammation. For Ailux's IL-4Rα area monitoring, the Phase 3 readout timeline (2028–2029) sets the window for any licensing or partnership conversations around next-generation IL-4R assets.

FcRn: Immunovant's Durability Signal in Graves' Disease

FOCUS AREA: FcRn · AUTOIMMUNE IgG DISEASES

Immunovant's proof-of-concept study of imeroprubart (IMVT-1402) in Graves' disease produced a striking durability signal: thyroid-stimulating hormone receptor antibodies (TRAb) remained suppressed six months after treatment discontinuation. This persistence of effect — well beyond the expected IgG recovery timeline after FcRn inhibition — suggests that deep IgG depletion may trigger a reset in the autoantibody repertoire, not merely a temporary suppression.

Immunovant is now running potentially registrational trials of imeroprubart across Graves' disease, myasthenia gravis, CIDP, and Sjogren's disease, with proof-of-concept data in cutaneous lupus and difficult-to-treat rheumatoid arthritis expected in 2026. The FcRn inhibitor class now has three approved agents in myasthenia gravis (efgartigimod/Vyvgart from argenx; rozanolixizumab/Rystiggo from UCB; nipocalimab), but expansion into non-MG indications is where the next wave of differentiation occurs.

BD signal: The durability signal in Graves' disease is particularly relevant for Ailux's FcRn area. If FcRn inhibitors can demonstrate disease modification (not just symptom control) in autoantibody-driven diseases, the class transitions from chronic therapy to potential curative intent — fundamentally changing the valuation framework for FcRn-targeting assets.

JHU CONCEPT TO HOLD

Estimands and the ICH E9(R1) Framework

Cabaletta's preconditioning-free data raises a clinical development question that maps directly to MF705 (Clinical Development): how do you define the treatment effect when the comparator arm uses lymphodepletion and the experimental arm does not? Under the ICH E9(R1) estimand framework, the "treatment policy" strategy — measuring the effect of the treatment strategy as assigned, regardless of what happens after randomization — is the default for most regulatory submissions. But in a PC-free vs. preconditioned comparison, the intercurrent event (preconditioning itself) is not a deviation from protocol — it is the protocol difference.

The correct estimand choice here is a "composite" strategy: define the treatment effect as the outcome of the complete regimen (PC-free rese-cel vs. preconditioned rese-cel), where the preconditioning is part of the treatment, not an intercurrent event. This framing preserves the clinical relevance of the comparison — the question is not "does rese-cel work?" but "does rese-cel work without the toxicity of conditioning?" That distinction between estimand strategies is exactly the kind of regulatory design choice that determines whether a BLA succeeds or stalls.
