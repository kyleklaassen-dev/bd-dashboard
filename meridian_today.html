In 1998, a medicinal chemist at Daiichi named Hideki Hyodo attached a cytotoxic warhead to a monoclonal antibody using a cleavable linker and a payload called exatecan. The conjugate failed. The linker was too unstable. The payload was too toxic at the required dose. The concept — an antibody that delivers chemotherapy directly into a tumor cell — sat dormant for nearly a decade. Nobody at Daiichi called it a dead end. They called it unfinished.

Twenty years later, a team led by Takeshi Ota redesigned the linker, swapped in deruxtecan (a topoisomerase I inhibitor), and engineered a drug-to-antibody ratio of 8 — double what the field considered safe. The resulting molecule, trastuzumab deruxtecan, became ENHERTU. Last Thursday, the FDA approved it for two new indications in early-stage HER2-positive breast cancer, placing it at both ends of the surgical treatment arc: before the knife and after. A single ADC now anchors the entire perioperative pathway in one of the most common aggressive breast cancer subtypes.

That same week, the FDA commissioner resigned. Three approvals — atezolizumab, ENHERTU, benralizumab — all cleared in the span of four days, while the agency's leadership fracture widened. The engine is running. The navigation system is offline. The tea is hot, and this morning's briefing is about what happens when the science outpaces the institution that governs it.

ENHERTU Closes the Loop: Two Approvals Anchor the HER2+ Perioperative Arc

FOCUS AREA: BIOPHARMA BD & PIPELINE · CLINICAL DEVELOPMENT

On May 15, the FDA approved fam-trastuzumab deruxtecan-nxki (ENHERTU, AstraZeneca / Daiichi Sankyo) for two separate indications in adults with HER2-positive early-stage breast cancer. The first covers neoadjuvant use — pre-surgical treatment to shrink tumors — based on DESTINY-Breast11. The second covers adjuvant use in patients with residual invasive disease after surgery, based on DESTINY-Breast05. Taken together, these approvals mean a single ADC now occupies both nodes of the perioperative treatment pathway — replacing anthracyclines pre-surgery and T-DM1 (Kadcyla, Roche) post-surgery.

The neoadjuvant result is structurally important beyond its headline pCR number. DESTINY-Breast11 used a three-arm design comparing T-DXd followed by taxane/trastuzumab/pertuzumab (THP) against the historical standard of dose-dense doxorubicin/cyclophosphamide (ddAC) followed by THP. The 67.3% pCR rate in the T-DXd arm means that two-thirds of patients achieved complete pathological response — no residual invasive cancer detectable in the breast or lymph nodes — without exposure to anthracyclines. The cardiotoxicity, myelosuppression, and long-term second-malignancy risk of anthracycline-based chemotherapy are well-established burdens. Eliminating that exposure from the neoadjuvant sequence is a meaningful quality-of-life win.

The adjuvant result from DESTINY-Breast05 is even cleaner from a clinical development standpoint. This was a direct head-to-head comparison: T-DXd versus T-DM1 in patients with residual invasive disease following neoadjuvant trastuzumab-based therapy. The HR of 0.47 for invasive disease-free survival translates to a 53% risk reduction — one of the largest treatment effects seen in adjuvant breast cancer trials this decade. The three-year IDFS rate was 92.4% for T-DXd versus 83.7% for T-DM1.

The Bystander Effect and the ADC Design Lesson

The mechanistic basis for ENHERTU's superiority over Kadcyla lies in its bystander effect. T-DXd's deruxtecan payload is membrane-permeable: once released inside a HER2-expressing tumor cell, it diffuses into neighboring cells regardless of their HER2 status. This kills HER2-low and even HER2-negative cells in the tumor microenvironment. T-DM1's emtansine payload, by contrast, is membrane-impermeable — it only kills the cell it enters. In a heterogeneous tumor (and nearly all tumors are heterogeneous), T-DXd's kill radius is larger. The design choice was not about potency per se — DM1 is potent — but about spatial reach.

For Ailux's BD conversations, the ENHERTU story is a case study in how linker-payload engineering — not just target selection — drives clinical outcomes. AI-native antibody design platforms can optimize binding affinity, epitope selection, and bispecific geometry, but the ADC field is demonstrating that the value chain extends to payload chemistry. The partnership opportunity is clear: Ailux's AI-designed binders paired with next-generation payloads from companies like Tubulis or Synaffix.

Unresolved question. Patients who fail to achieve pCR with neoadjuvant T-DXd (32.7% in DESTINY-Breast11) now qualify for adjuvant T-DXd under the DESTINY-Breast05 label. But the adjuvant trial enrolled T-DXd-naive patients. What is the interstitial lung disease (ILD) risk when T-DXd is administered sequentially — neoadjuvant then adjuvant? ILD rates in DESTINY-Breast05 were manageable (~2% grade 3+), but that was a single-exposure population. This is an open pharmacovigilance question that will take years of real-world data to answer.

The Approval Machine Without a Pilot: Makary Exits, Diamantas Steps In

FOCUS AREA: REGULATORY & CORPORATE STRATEGY

On May 12, FDA Commissioner Marty Makary resigned after thirteen months in the role. The departure, reported by STAT News, was precipitated by White House frustration over the agency's handling of flavored vape applications, compounded by ongoing turmoil in the drug and biologics divisions and Makary's controversial decision to reject Moderna's mRNA flu vaccine application over control arm design. Kyle Diamantas, the FDA's deputy commissioner for food — a legal and policy specialist with no drug regulatory background — was named acting commissioner.

The timing produces a striking juxtaposition. During the week of May 12–15, the FDA approved three significant therapies: atezolizumab for ctDNA-directed adjuvant bladder cancer (the first ctDNA-guided approval in the solid tumor adjuvant setting), ENHERTU for two early-stage HER2+ breast cancer indications, and benralizumab for hypereosinophilic syndrome. Three approvals, one resignation, four days. The scientific review apparatus continued to function at full capacity even as the agency's executive layer fractured.

Porter's Five Forces Applied to the FDA Gatekeeper

Corporate Strategy offers a useful lens here. In Porter's Five Forces framework, a regulatory agency is not a competitor — it is a structural force that shapes the threat of entry and the bargaining power of suppliers (where the supplier is the drug developer and the customer is the payer/patient system). When the gatekeeper's capacity diminishes — through leadership vacuums, DOGE-era staffing cuts, or policy inconsistency — the effect is not that entry becomes easier. It becomes less predictable. Predictability is the asset that biotech companies price into their development timelines. Unpredictability raises the risk premium on late-stage clinical programs.

The practical downstream effects are already visible. REGENXBIO has signaled it is waiting for FDA leadership to stabilize before filing its DMD gene therapy BLA. Companies with complex BLA submissions — combination biologics, gene therapies requiring coordinated CBER/CDER review, and novel endpoints like ctDNA-guided treatment — face the highest uncertainty. For Ailux, whose first-in-human programs target 2027, the relevant question is whether CDER's IND review infrastructure is affected by staffing gaps at the reviewing-division level, independent of who sits in the commissioner's chair.

The search for a permanent commissioner is reportedly stalled. Former commissioner Stephen Hahn and former acting commissioner Brett Giroir are named as potential candidates, but neither has been formally nominated. The pharmaceutical industry's preference — expressed through BIO and PhRMA lobbying — is for a commissioner with drug-review experience who can restore Makary's stated goal of a single-pivotal-trial pathway while avoiding the policy lurches that defined his tenure.

Fasenra in HES: How AstraZeneca Turns One Antibody Into a Franchise

FOCUS AREA: PIPELINE & BD STRATEGY

On May 14, the FDA approved benralizumab (Fasenra, AstraZeneca) for adults and adolescents (12+) with hypereosinophilic syndrome (HES) without an identifiable non-hematologic secondary cause, based on the NATRON Phase III trial. Fasenra reduced the risk of first HES flare by 65% versus placebo. The approval extends Fasenra's label from severe eosinophilic asthma and eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) into a third eosinophil-mediated rare disease.

HES is a heterogeneous group of disorders characterized by persistent peripheral blood eosinophilia above 1,500/µL with resulting end-organ damage — cardiac, dermatologic, pulmonary, and neurologic. The current standard of care is chronic corticosteroids and hydroxyurea, both carrying significant long-term toxicity. Adding a targeted anti-IL-5Rα biologic with a clean safety profile — benralizumab depletes eosinophils via ADCC-mediated killing rather than just blocking proliferation — represents a meaningful therapeutic advance in a population with few options.

The strategic story, though, is about franchise architecture. AstraZeneca has systematically converted benralizumab's broad anti-eosinophilic biology into distinct regulatory indications, each with its own clinical trial and label. Severe eosinophilic asthma (2017), EGPA (2024), HES (2026). The molecule hasn't changed. The clinical question has changed each time — and each answer opens a new commercial channel. This is the biologic equivalent of platform leverage: develop once, file repeatedly.

For Ailux, the Fasenra model is instructive for portfolio planning. An AI-designed antibody with validated pharmacology against a single target can generate a multi-indication franchise if the underlying biology spans diseases. The anti-eosinophilic pathway (IL-5/IL-5R) maps across asthma, EGPA, HES, chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps, and eosinophilic esophagitis — all of which are eosinophil-driven but regulated as separate indications. Ailux's own anti-fibrotic TL1A antibody faces a similar franchise opportunity across IBD, fibrotic lung disease, and potentially systemic sclerosis. The design lesson from AstraZeneca: start with the broadest validated biology, then file indication by indication.

The Gold Standard on Display: Head-to-Head Trials and What They Actually Prove

CLINICAL DEVELOPMENT · JHU INTEGRATION

DESTINY-Breast05 is an unusually clean example of a comparative effectiveness trial — and it is worth dissecting through the lens of ICH E9(R1) estimand thinking, which is the framework your Clinical Development course uses to evaluate what a trial is actually measuring versus what it appears to measure.

The traditional question a trial answers is: "Does Drug A work better than Drug B?" But the estimand framework asks you to be more precise. What is the treatment effect of interest? In DESTINY-Breast05, the treatment policy estimand is the difference in IDFS between T-DXd and T-DM1 in patients randomized as treated, regardless of what happened after randomization (treatment switches, discontinuations, dose modifications). The HR of 0.47 reflects this intent-to-treat population. But if a patient discontinued T-DXd due to ILD (the drug's primary toxicity) and crossed to T-DM1, the treatment policy estimand still counts that patient in the T-DXd arm. The estimand does not tell you what T-DXd would do in patients who tolerate the full course — that would require a hypothetical estimand (what would IDFS be if ILD never occurred?).

This distinction matters practically. The HR of 0.47 is a population-level effect. For individual patients — the ones sitting in oncology clinics — the question is: "Will I tolerate this drug long enough to benefit?" The composite estimand (treatment policy) and the individual patient estimand (tolerance-conditional) can diverge. Understanding which one a trial reports is the difference between reading a clinical paper and reading through it.

The second Clinical Development lesson from DESTINY-Breast05 is the power of a head-to-head active comparator design. Most pivotal trials in oncology compare Drug A to placebo plus standard of care, because beating placebo is the regulatory bar. Head-to-head trials against an active comparator — here, T-DXd vs T-DM1 — are commercially harder (you might lose) but regulatorily and clinically more informative. The FDA increasingly favors direct comparisons when a standard of care exists. AstraZeneca's willingness to run this trial, and the magnitude of the result, gives ENHERTU a commercial advantage that placebo-controlled trials cannot: prescribers can see exactly how much better it is than the drug they were already using.

CHENGYU · 成语

厚积薄发

hòu jī bó fā

"Accumulate thickly, release thinly." To build deeply over a long period and unleash the result at the decisive moment.

ENHERTU is a 厚积薄发 (hòu jī bó fā) story. Two decades of ADC chemistry failures — unstable linkers, toxic payloads, low DAR ratios — were the 厚积 (hòu jī, "thick accumulation"): years of knowledge compounding without a breakthrough product. The deruxtecan redesign was the 薄发 (bó fā, "thin release"): a single, decisive engineering solution that turned the accumulated platform into a drug that now anchors an entire treatment pathway. The same pattern applies to Ailux's fundraise — years of partnership revenue, AI platform maturation, and pipeline credentialing, now releasing into a first external round as the tariff environment softens.

CONCEPT TO HOLD

The Machine Outlasts the Operator

Three FDA approvals cleared in four days while the commissioner was resigning. The scientific review infrastructure — the thousands of reviewers, statisticians, and clinical pharmacologists who evaluate NDAs and BLAs — continued to function at full capacity independent of executive leadership. The same pattern holds in drug development: the platform outlasts the person who built it. ENHERTU survived its original chemist. Benralizumab outlasted its first clinical champion. The institution matters more than any single leader. But institutions without leaders eventually drift. The question is not whether the machine can run without a pilot. It can. The question is where it ends up.

Three approvals, one resignation, and the largest oncology data event of the year arriving in eleven days. The science doesn't wait for the org chart to catch up. Neither should you.
