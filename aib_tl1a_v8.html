The landscape in brief

Anti-TL1A monotherapy is now a four-way Phase 3 race: tulisokibart (Merck), afimkibart (Roche), duvakitug (Sanofi/Teva), with SPY002 (Spyre) close behind. The mechanism is validated; the lane is crowded. The question that is still open — and that defines value from here — is whether combining TL1A blockade with IL-23p19 blockade in a single molecule produces additive efficacy beyond the best monotherapy. No bispecific has yet answered it in patients.

That single unanswered question is where value concentrates. The asset that answers it first — with a manufacturable, long-acting subcutaneous profile and a clean safety read — steps into a deal comp set anchored by Merck's $10.8B purchase of Prometheus and Roche's $7.1B purchase of Telavant.1

The frontier in one line: IL-23p19 monotherapy is approved and durable in IBD; TL1A monotherapy is validated in Phase 2 and now in Phase 3; the dual-mechanism bispecific is unproven and uncrowded. The prize is closing the gap between today's ~15-20% deep-remission ceiling and the 30-40% dual blockade is hypothesized to reach.

What makes an asset valuable here

Before naming any single program, it is worth stating plainly what creates — or destroys — value for an asset in this space. These are the axes any TL1A/IL-23 asset is judged on:

What the field is saying. Independent commentary frames anti-TL1A as a genuine new class for UC while cautioning that remission rates remain modest and that durability and positioning are still to be proven2 — which is precisely why a mechanism that lifts the efficacy ceiling, rather than adding another single-pathway option, is the high-value move.

The field has reached a remission ceiling. Even the best current agents put only ~15-20% of patients into deep remission; 40-50% fail or lose response within five years; and after exhausting anti-TNF, vedolizumab, ustekinumab and JAK inhibitors, an estimated 150-200K US ulcerative-colitis patients reach the point of colectomy. The unmet need, stated precisely, is not the number of options — it is the depth and durability of remission each option delivers.3

Why a bispecific is a rational response. Patients fail current biologics because blocking a single pathway leaves an escape route open (Section 2 shows the data). TL1A blockade addresses inflammation and the fibro-stromal remodeling behind stricturing disease; IL-23p19 blockade addresses the Th17 maintenance biology. Hitting both with one molecule is the mechanism-level answer to a ceiling that single pathways have not broken — and it is the reason AbbVie pairs its anti-TL1A (ABBV-701) with SKYRIZI (IL-23) as a two-drug regimen.4

2 · The scientific thesis

Step 1 — Target engagement is not the same as efficacy

Anti-TL1A antibodies achieve near-complete target engagement — tulisokibart suppresses free soluble TL1A by ~90%, afimkibart by ~92% — yet clinical remission lands at ~26%. In ARTEMIS-UC, tulisokibart produced 26% remission versus 1% on placebo at Week 12 (and 32% vs 11% in a genetic-biomarker-selected subgroup).5 The molecule is binding and neutralizing its target as designed; the disease nonetheless persists in three-quarters of patients. That gap between engagement and outcome is the problem the field has to solve.

Step 2 — Why the gap exists: the escape pathway

The leading explanation is that TL1A blockade leaves IL-23-dependent Th17 activation and epithelial-restitution failure untouched. The non-responder biology supports this: a large share of anti-TL1A non-responders escape through Th17 routes, while anti-IL-23 non-responders tend to escape through Th1 / IL-12-independent IFN-γ — two largely non-overlapping escape maps. Afimkibart's own serum proteomics make the point from the other direction: it downregulates Th17, myeloid and fibrotic pathways6, confirming TL1A's reach into fibrosis but also showing where a second mechanism is still needed. Two non-overlapping escape routes is the mechanistic definition of a combination opportunity.

Step 3 — Why IL-23p19, and what is already proven

IL-23p19 is the right second arm for two reasons. Biologically, it closes the Th17 escape route while preserving IL-12/Th1 immunity (unlike p40). Commercially and regulatorily, it is already validated in IBD: mirikizumab (Omvoh) is FDA-approved in UC (2023) and Crohn's (2025) with four-year durability data, risankizumab (Skyrizi) is approved in Crohn's and UC, and guselkumab is approved — three agents with broadly comparable efficacy.7 So of the two arms in a TL1A×IL-23p19 bispecific, one is fully de-risked (IL-23p19 — approved, durable), one is partially de-risked (TL1A — positive Phase 2, now in Phase 3 but not yet approved), and the combination itself is the only unproven element. The work still to be done is the additive-efficacy readout — not the validation of either pathway alone.

The testable conclusion. If the engagement-to-remission gap is caused by a non-overlapping IL-23-dependent escape pathway, then blocking both pathways with one molecule should convert engagement into remission — the basis for the 30-40% deep-remission hypothesis. It is falsifiable: if dual blockade does not beat the best monospecific, the thesis fails. RO7837195 (a p40×TL1A bispecific) and MT-251 are the first programs positioned to test it; neither has reported additive efficacy yet.

Reading the afimkibart 'miss' correctly. TUSCANY-2 is widely described as a failure, but the detail matters and is instructive. Its primary endpoint — clinical remission by total Mayo score — was not statistically significant (26/23/24% across doses vs 12% placebo). Its secondary endpoint using the modified Mayo score — the endpoint tulisokibart, duvakitug and the FDA now use — was significant (30/35/32% vs 12%).15 The lesson is not that the mechanism failed; it is that endpoint choice can decide whether a trial reads as success or failure, and that afimkibart's true efficacy is in the same band as its competitors. Roche is continuing development on that basis.

Biomarkers — a strategy, stated honestly

Three biomarker tiers operate here: predictive / companion-diagnostic (e.g., the genetic response signature used in the tulisokibart program); escape markers (Oncostatin M / the OSM-OSMR axis); and pharmacodynamic monitoring (soluble TL1A, fecal calprotectin, CRP).

A direction worth pursuing, not a claim of fact. High mucosal OSM is associated with non-response to anti-TNF and other single-cytokine blockade in IBD17. That makes OSM a rational selection marker for identifying the escape population a TL1A monotherapy leaves behind — and a strategy a dual-blockade asset could build around to target patients a companion-diagnostic-enriched monotherapy excludes. This is a hypothesis the biology supports, not a validated companion diagnostic; pursuing it would give a BD team and a development team a concrete way to define and own an addressable segment.

4 · Molecular design & manufacturing

  • Half-life engineering. XmAb942 (~74 d) shows Fc/FcRn optimization can roughly triple a native antibody's half-life; MT-251 is built on pH-sensitive binding + FcRn recycling for the same end. A bispecific that does not match this loses on convenience even at efficacy parity.

  • Format & valency. Approaches span IgG-like dual-targeting antibodies, single-domain (VHH) constructs, and fixed combinations of two separate antibodies (e.g., Spyre's SPY230 = SPY002 + SPY003). A single molecule wins on payer story and cost of goods; a combination is faster to the clinic but carries two-asset cost and complexity.

  • Arm selection (p19 vs p40). p19 preserves IL-12/Th1 immunity and inherits the approved mirikizumab/risankizumab precedent; p40 (RO7837195) also blocks IL-12 — broader, but with the redundancy and historical safety questions of the ustekinumab era.

  • The manufacturing reality. Single-molecule bispecifics carry materially higher CMC complexity than a monoclonal antibody — chain mispairing, aggregation, lower yields, harder comparability. This is a cost and timeline burden, and part of why the bispecific clinic is still thin despite obvious interest.

5 · Safety & immunogenicity

For a dual-immunosuppression strategy, safety carries as much weight as efficacy. The backdrop is encouraging for specific, evidenced reasons:

  • The IL-23p19 arm is reassuring. The anti-IL-23p19 class (risankizumab, mirikizumab, guselkumab) has, across large approved programs, shown low rates of serious infection and no malignancy signal — a cleaner profile than anti-TNF. The IL-23 arm therefore adds efficacy without importing the safety cost of older mechanisms.18

  • The TL1A arm has held up so far. Through 50 weeks in ARTEMIS-UC, tulisokibart showed no new safety signals: infections were predominantly upper-respiratory (Week-50 rates 37% at 100mg, 46% at 250mg), with no serious infections and no discontinuations due to infection.19

The open question. Whether blocking TL1A and IL-23 simultaneously raises infection risk beyond either alone is genuinely unknown — there is no human dual-blockade safety dataset yet. The biology gives reason for cautious optimism (the pathways are partly non-overlapping and IL-23 blockade is well tolerated), but this is the first endpoint a partner's diligence will examine, and it will not be settled until the bispecifics read out.

9 · Competitive geography

A structural feature Western-centric analyses miss: the TL1A×IL-23 bispecific field is disproportionately China-originated — LaNova (LQ080, out-licensed ex-China to Zymeworks), Simcere (SIM0709, to Boehringer Ingelheim), plus NovaMab, Elpiscience, Biocad and Qyuns. The dominant deal pattern is a Western pharma licensing-in a China-originated bispecific (the BI–Simcere precedent). For any asset in this space, the implication is that the competitive set — and the likely acquirers — are global, and that Western-quality data packages and clear IP are what convert a China-originated molecule into a premium transaction.

10 · Scenarios & what could go wrong

Scenario (ATLAS-UC × first bispecific signal)Prob.Implication
A — both positive~45%bispecific premium rises; partnering urgency increases
B — ATLAS hits, bispecific disappoints~25%monospecific dominance reinforced; bispecific thesis weakened
C — ATLAS misses, bispecific positive~10%re-rating toward dual blockade; largest upside
D — both disappoint~20%class-wide doubt
The scenario that destroys value. If the first bispecific reads (RO7837195, MT-251) show no additive efficacy over monospecifics, and ATLAS-UC succeeds (making tulisokibart standard of care), then a bispecific that does not beat a single antibody has no buyer — and a preclinical entrant's value approaches zero. This is the ~25% Scenario-B world. The defense is a clinical design that reads additive efficacy early and cheaply (a focused Ph1b proof-of-concept), so a program can stop or pivot before committing Phase 2 capital.

Second-order effects worth tracking. The value is often in the reaction, not the event. If ATLAS-UC hits: Merck gains pricing/negotiating leverage and sets the UC anchor → time-pressured buyers (e.g., those facing biosimilar erosion) feel more urgency → holders of monospecific assets reposition on safety/convenience → integrated players accelerate their own programs. The practical read is that the most active business-development window tends to open 3-6 months after a defining readout, when the pressure is felt but the next question is still open.
